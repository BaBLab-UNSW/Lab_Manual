Brain & Body Lab Manual

Section 9: Theoretical Orientation

Where we stand, and why it matters for how you work here

Most of you arrive having been trained in a broadly essentialist view of development: that psychological categories — basic emotions, temperament types, attachment styles, cognitive stages — are natural kinds with a fixed biological essence, expressed on a maturational timetable that unfolds largely independent of an individual child’s learning history. This is a theoretical stance, not a fact about how the mind works. The lab follows a different theoretical stance.

This lab works from a constructionist view of the brain and mind, in the tradition associated with Lisa Feldman Barrett’s Theory of Constructed Emotion (TCE) and the broader predictive-processing framework. The core claim of this viewpoint is that psychological states (including emotions foremost), are not triggered by dedicated neural modules that switch on when the right stimulus appears. They are constructed in the moment, out of more basic ingredients (interoception, core affect, and prior conceptual knowledge), by a brain that is continuously predicting and updating rather than reacting to the environment. What looks like a discrete, universal category from the outside (fear, anger, a “shy” temperament, an “insecure” attachment style) is, on this view, a population of highly variable instances that a perceiver — including a researcher — has learned to group together. There is no reliable one-to-one mapping between a physiological signature (like brain circuit or microbe) and an emotion category. This is the “degeneracy” and “many-to-one/one-to-one” problem that the essentialist view has trouble explaining.

Why does this matter? Because being explicit about your theoretical stance, which guides your assumptions, and decisions, is critical. Too often in Psychology, we miss this step completely, and are thus applying these assumptions and letting them guide our research questions and analyses uncritically.

A note on terminology: “constructionist,” not “constructivist.” You’ll hear these words used interchangeably outside this lab, and it’s worth being precise, because you likely already know one of them from elsewhere in your training. Constructivism (e.g., Piaget, Vygotsky) is a theory of learning — an account of how an individual builds their own understanding of the world through experience (Piaget), or through language and social interaction with others (Vygotsky). It’s a claim about how knowledge gets acquired over development. Constructionism, in the sense this lab uses it, is a different kind of claim about how a psychological category (e.g., an emotion) is itself assembled in the moment out of more basic ingredients (core affect, interoception, prior concepts), rather than existing as a discrete, pre-formed module waiting to be triggered. It sits closer to the sociological tradition of social constructionism (categories like gender or emotion are built, not carved at nature’s joints) than to Piagetian constructivism — though unlike strict social constructionism, it grounds that building in biology rather than treating the category as a purely social invention.

What this means for development: we do not treat development as an essentialist unfolding of pre-formed categories on a maturational clock. We treat it as a story of concept learning — how a child’s predictive brain, through embodied and social experience, calibrates the categories it uses to make sense of its own internal states and other people’s. Individual differences reflect different learning histories.

What this looks like in practice in this lab

  • We try to be careful with language, avoiding phrases like “the fear circuit,” “hard-wired,” or “the brain’s anger response”.
  • We do not treat facial expression, physiology, or self-report as separately capable of “revealing” a true underlying emotion category. Each is one noisy channel into a constructed state; disagreement between them is expected, not a measurement failure.
  • We ask what conceptual, contextual, and interoceptive ingredients might explain a finding.
  • We treat variability within a category (across people, across contexts, across development) as data, not noise to be averaged away.
  • This logic extends past emotion. We’re skeptical of research programs that go looking for a single biological “essence” of a psychological category — a specific “depression microbe,” a dedicated “anxiety circuit” — for the same reason we’re skeptical of a dedicated fear module: complex psychological categories are unlikely to map cleanly onto one isolatable biological marker.
  • We’re cautious about deterministic claims about biology-behavior links
  • For the same reason, when we study adversity, we tend to prefer a “features” framework over the popular threat/deprivation dimensional model (McLaughlin, Sheridan, and colleagues). Threat/deprivation sorts children’s experiences into two broad categories, but real adversity — abuse, neglect, poverty — rarely separates cleanly into one bucket or the other (Pollak & Smith describe this as trying to pull apart a grey sweater looking for the black threads and the white threads). We favor measuring specific features of an environment instead — caregiver predictability, stability, social support, and how a child construes their own circumstances — because it’s more consistent with a constructionist view: adversity’s effects aren’t a fixed categorical exposure, but something the developing brain constructs meaning from, out of the granular features of experience actually available to it. (Pollak, S.D., & Smith, K.E. (2021). Thinking clearly about biology and childhood adversity: Next steps for continued progress. Perspectives on Psychological Science, 16(6), 1473–1477.)
  • We hold this stance as an empirically motivated position, not dogma — you are expected to be able to explain why we hold it and to engage seriously with the essentialist literature you’ll still be reading, teaching, and reviewing for others. Perhaps you decide you prefer the essentialist framework - fine, but you still need to know about constructionism and defend your stance against constructionist critiques.
