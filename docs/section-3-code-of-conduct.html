Section 3: Code of Conduct

3.1 Essential Policies

UNSW Code of Conduct and Values

UNSW has a Code of Conduct and Values, which applies to staff and students across all teaching, learning, and research activities, and a separate Student Code of Conduct process for handling breaches. Please take the time to familiarise yourself with both before you start in the lab.

3.2 Scientific Integrity

Reproducible Research

The BABLab is committed to performing reproducible research. Reproducible research means that if you gave someone your raw data and analysis code, they should be able to reproduce your results exactly. If they can’t, it suggests that something is wrong (e.g., a mistake in the pipeline) or that you didn’t properly document the data collection, cleaning, or analysis process. Neither of these is good. Therefore, we take steps to make sure all aspects of data collection, cleaning, and analysis are extremely well documented.

For results to be reproducible, you need to be organised and document everything! Take detailed notes on your study design, save all citations for questionnaires you use, and if you make up a questionnaire yourself, document that it was custom-made for the study, so that no one spends hours down the track looking for a citation that doesn’t exist. Keep notes on recruitment methods, inclusion/exclusion criteria, participant scheduling, all research protocols, and any changes to protocols that occur during the study. If something goes wrong or something unusual happens, document that too. Document scanner/equipment parameters and the software versions used to collect data. Before you start collecting data, build a wiki page that documents every component of the study and update it as a living document throughout. This wiki should contain everything anyone would ever need to know about your study.

Once you’ve collected data, take detailed notes on data cleaning and analysis. Even if you’ve preregistered your study design (which you very likely will), you still need to document this stage, as you may move away from your preregistered choices for a variety of reasons — you need to know and record what those reasons are. Write down how you did things, in what order, from pre-processing through to statistical tests. Your code should also be clearly commented, so it’s understandable months later by you or by an outsider. It is highly encouraged that you use version control (e.g., Git with GitHub) to track code changes and share code with others.

In sum: write copious notes on your study design and measures before you start, and put all of it in the wiki. Write beautifully annotated analysis code (e.g., in R Markdown or Quarto) and put it on GitHub. There is nothing more satisfying in science (or life) than a well-organised study.

Authorship

Like other labs, we follow the APA guidelines with respect to authorship:

“Authorship credit should reflect the individual’s contribution to the study. An author is considered anyone involved with initial research design, data collection and analysis, manuscript drafting, and final approval. However, the following do not necessarily qualify for authorship: providing funding or resources, mentorship, or contributing research but not helping with the publication itself. The primary author assumes responsibility for the publication, making sure that the data are accurate, that all deserving authors have been credited, that all authors have given their approval to the final draft, and handles responses to inquiries after the manuscript is published.”

This aligns with UNSW’s Research Authorship, Publication and Dissemination Policy, which lab members should also read. We use the CRediT authorship taxonomy to record specific contributions. Issues with authorship should be raised with Bridget — and to head off disputes before they start, any new project should agree an authorship order (or at least the principles for deciding one) in writing at the outset, revisited if the project’s shape changes substantially.

Old Projects

If an HDR candidate or postdoc drives a project and/or collects a dataset but does not analyse it, write it up, or actively work on it within a reasonable time frame after data collection ends, Bridget may discuss handing the project off to someone who can complete it, to expedite publication. If someone no longer wishes to work on a project, or no longer wants to be an author, Bridget will reassign the project. This policy exists to prevent data — especially expensive data, e.g., fMRI — from remaining unpublished. Remember the lab philosophy: “do good science that makes a difference.” We can’t make a difference if research isn’t disseminated.

Responsible Conduct of Research

All research in the BABLab is conducted under the Australian Code for the Responsible Conduct of Research, the national framework covering honesty, rigour, transparency, fairness, and accountability in research. All HDR candidates must complete UNSW’s Responsible Conduct of Research (RCR) training, and Bridget, as supervisor, is responsible for guiding, mentoring, and monitoring that training. Everyone in the lab — not just HDR candidates — should read and understand the Code. If you have a concern about a possible breach of the Code (by anyone, at any level), you can raise it with Bridget, or speak confidentially with one of UNSW’s Research Integrity Advisors, who exist specifically to advise on this without it needing to go through your supervisor first.

3.3 Human Subjects Research

Adherence to approved human research ethics approvals is essential, and non-adherence can lead to severe consequences for the entire lab (i.e., we may lose permission to run any research on human participants). Part of respecting our participants means we must strictly adhere to the approved protocol for each project. All lab members must read the ethics approval documents and consent forms for any project they’re working on. This is also a great way to familiarise yourself with a project, and you’re welcome to read the approvals for other lab projects you’re not working on, to learn more about them. If you’re not named on a project’s ethics approval (e.g., a volunteer with the lab), you cannot run participants, look at the data, analyse the data, or be involved with the project in any way.

At UNSW, human research ethics approval is granted by a Human Research Ethics Advisory Panel (HREAP) — there are several discipline-based panels, and the relevant one is determined by the Coordinating Chief Investigator’s School or Faculty. Applications are submitted as a Human Research Ethics Application (HREA). Lab members must complete the human research ethics training specified for the protocols they’re listed on. Speak to your lab manager about which protocols you’ll be listed on and what training is required, plus how to access and document it.

If you’re starting your own project, you’ll be expected to write your own HREA (see Bridget and your lab manager for help). If you’re starting a study within an existing project, you’ll need to write an amendment to that project’s approval. If you’re joining an existing project, you’ll need to be added to that project’s approved personnel list (see your HDR, postdoc, or lab manager mentor about this). You must have ethics approval before collecting, looking at, or using any human participant data.

Part of human research ethics training covers reporting of incidental or unexpected events. If a participant falls ill, becomes very upset, has an accident with lab equipment, is injured, or is otherwise adversely affected by participating in a study, tell Bridget so we can report this to the relevant HREAP and/or funding agencies as required.

If your research involves Aboriginal or Torres Strait Islander participants, communities, or data, additional obligations apply under the AIATSIS Code of Ethics and NHMRC guidelines — see Section 1.3 and talk to Bridget and Nura Gili before designing the study, not after ethics submission.
