Brain & Body Lab Manual

Section 2: Expectations and Responsibilities

2.1 Everyone

Big Picture

Sometimes science can be hard, and the rewards (interesting research findings, papers, etc.) can be few and far between. However, science is always fun when we make the lab a great place to be. In the BABLab, we want everyone to have a positive experience that is free from hostility, so that people can do their best, most productive science. Great labs don’t just happen, they are built. Here is what we can all do to help:

  • Do work that you are proud of.
  • Work carefully, and strategically. Do not rush.
  • Respect our participants. Whether they are families, undergrads, or adults from the community, they are giving up their time to be involved in research. Always act professionally with participants over the phone and in person, and do not leave them waiting. Keep in mind that participants may have different political and religious beliefs, sexual orientations, family compositions, and life experiences to you and/or your friends and family. Please conduct yourself in a way that respects their unique situation and personhood.
  • Respect our child participants. In addition to the above point, remember that children are to be respected as much as adults. Please be aware that even well-meaning adults can sound patronising sometimes when speaking with children. At the risk of sounding patronising myself, comments like “cool shoes” are fine, but comments like “you are so cute” are not in this context.
  • Respect the data and the people who collected it. Data collection, especially in developmental populations, is hard. People put real effort into it — coming in on weekends, after hours, on holidays. You might be analysing data that you did not have to collect. That is a pretty great situation, so make sure you treat that data in the same way you would your own.
  • Respect your lab mates: their culture, religion, beliefs, sexual orientation, and personality quirks. Science is for everyone.
  • Respect yourself: your need for time off, your happiness, your other commitments. You are responsible for constructing your own boundaries.
  • Be supportive of your lab mates. We are a team. Science is about collaboration, not competition.
  • Be forgiving of yourself and one another. Academic environments can be challenging. We all make mistakes, and not everyone is on their A-game every day. Whatever happens, try to be understanding, forgive, and move on.
  • Tension or hostility in the lab affects everyone, and behaving in a bullying, intimidating, rude, or disrespectful way will not be tolerated. I expect everyone to be mature and professional while in the lab — this is a workplace and should be treated as one. If there are any problems which cannot be dealt with in the lab, please tell Bridget. If the concern involves Bridget herself, or you’d simply rather not raise it with her directly, you can instead contact `[UNSW Complaints (https://www.unsw.edu.au/complaints), or use UNSW’s confidential Complaints Management and Investigation channels.
  • Be honest with yourself and others. This means that if you notice a mistake, however embarrassing, admit it, correct it, and move on.
  • Check everything several times. Then get someone else to check it. Then check it again. It’s ok to make mistakes, but mistakes shouldn’t be because of carelessness or rushed work.
  • Under no circumstances is it ok to plagiarise, tamper with data, make up data, or omit data. If you are feeling pressured to succeed and think that is interfering with your judgement, reach out to Bridget so you can talk about it. Pressure is something we all face and is not an excuse to engage in research misconduct (see Section 3).
  • If you are struggling with health and/or unhappiness, or you see someone else struggling, please come and see me (Bridget). Everyone goes through rough patches, and we want to help each other out. UNSW also has free, confidential support: the Psychology & Wellness Service for students, and the Employee Assistance Program (via Converge International) for staff and their immediate family, which is free and available any time.
  • Have a life outside of the lab, take care of your mental and physical health, and don’t ever feel bad for taking time off work — and don’t make others feel bad for doing that either.

Small Picture

There are a few day-to-day things to keep in mind to keep the lab running smoothly.

Remote and hybrid work

When we’re not all in the same room, it’s even more important to check Teams chat and email regularly and be available to respond to messages, and to set up virtual meetings when needed. Keep participant-facing conduct professional in virtual meetings the same way you would in person — appropriate background, and appropriate dress if participants might see you.

General

  • If you’re sick, please take time off, stay home, and take care of yourself. Just message the lab manager or me (Bridget) to let us know.
  • If you know you might be unwell (e.g., you have minor surgery scheduled and don’t know how well you’ll recover), play it safe and reschedule your appointments ahead of time.
  • Working in developmental populations means work times need to be flexible. If you are collecting data or recruiting participants, you may be expected to work some weekends and holidays. We will share that burden as evenly as possible, and where this applies to paid staff, hours will be tracked and managed in line with your employment conditions (see Hours below) — talk to Bridget or the lab manager about time-in-lieu arrangements before, not after, taking on extra hours.
  • Outside of data collection and recruitment, you are not expected to work or come into the lab on weekends or holidays, and you are not expected to stay late at night.
  • You are expected to get your work done to a high standard, in whatever time of day best suits your work style. That said, being in lab some of the time is important for the lab and for morale, so you shouldn’t work remotely all of the time.
  • All HDR candidates, postdocs, research assistants, and paid employees (e.g., lab managers) are expected to attend lab meetings when their schedules allow. We will set a time for lab meeting each term that best fits everyone’s schedule.
  • Volunteers and other students are expected to attend lab meeting when it suits their schedule and interest.
  • Make sure the lab is locked if no one is inside, turn off the lights if you’re the last one leaving, and water the plants if they’re looking sad.
  • Keep the lab tidy. Eating in lab is fine, but clean up food waste, crumbs, and spills. Put lab equipment back where you found it. Keep common areas uncluttered.
  • Lab is a shared space. Unless you have been assigned a permanent desk, do not permanently claim a space. Use space when it’s available and remove your belongings when you leave so others can use it.
  • Dress code is casual, neat, and approachable. When interacting with participants or presenting your work, don’t wear pyjamas, dirty clothes, clothes with holes in them, or clothes that are see-through. Jeans are fine, and even active wear is fine when you’re collecting data (you might end up on the floor with a toddler). If you want to dress up, that’s fine too — as long as you’re comfortable and approachable.
  • When you are running participants, show up 15–20 minutes early to set everything up.
  • Be on time for your meetings: respect that others have busy schedules and everyone’s time is valuable.
  • If you need to cancel a meeting, please do so as early as possible so that other meetings/activities can be arranged in its place

2.2 Principal Investigator

All of the above, and I will…

  • Support your scientific development and be responsive to your emotional needs.
  • Give you feedback on a timely basis, including on project ideas, conference posters, talks, manuscripts, figures, and grants.
  • Be available in person and via Teams on a regular basis, including regular meetings to discuss your research.
  • Support your career development in whatever way I can (e.g., introducing you to other researchers, promoting your work at talks, writing recommendation letters, and supporting your attendance at conferences as finances permit).
  • Help you prepare for the next step of your career, whether it’s a postdoc, a faculty job, or a job outside academia.

2.3 Postdocs

All of the above, and you will also be expected to…

  • Develop your own independent line of research.
  • Create, submit, respond to, and monitor adherence to UNSW ethics for your research projects
  • Create publicly accessible Wiki’s (hosted on Github) for each of your research projects (see Bridget for examples)
  • Create Standard Operating Procedures for every component of your study, link them to the Wiki, and make them publicly available (on Github)
  • Learn to code in R, and establish data cleaning and analysis scripts in R, that you comprehensively annotate and store in Github (linking them to published manuscripts where appropriate)
  • Curate your datasets for public use at the completion of your research project, and where allowed, store them on Github.
  • Collect data for ongoing projects in the lab, including group projects. Although postdocs’ data collection responsibilities are lower than others in the lab, taking part in data collection for group projects is everyone’s responsibility.
  • Write and submit scientific papers (including review papers) in a timely manner.
  • Help train and mentor students in the lab (both undergraduate and HDR).
  • Seek out opportunities to present your work (e.g., at departmental events, in other labs, at conferences).
  • Engage in networking opportunities (e.g., chair a symposium, attend other lab meetings, volunteer as a student representative at conferences).
  • Apply for fellowships and grants (e.g., an ARC DECRA, an NHMRC Investigator Grant, a UNSW Scientia Postdoctoral Fellowship, or equivalent). Writing grants is an excellent exercise for finding direction in your research and identifying gaps in your training. It will also give you greater autonomy in the lab, help your CV, and help the lab (by freeing up funds and modelling the process for other students). Please see Bridget early about identifying grant mechanisms to target and for example applications.
  • Apply for jobs (academic or otherwise) when you’re ready, after discussing your progress with Bridget. Postdoctoral training can lead to many different jobs — academia, industry, and elsewhere. Any of those paths are fine with me (Bridget). Please keep me abreast of your thinking around potential jobs and I will support your career goals however I can.

2.4 HDR Candidates (PhD / MPhil)

All of the above, and you will also be expected to…

  • Develop your research project. This includes knowing the literature like the back of your hand. Take ownership of your project.
  • Create, submit, respond to, and monitor adherence to UNSW ethics for your research projects
  • Create publicly accessible Wiki’s (hosted on Github) for each of your research projects (see Bridget for examples)
  • Create Standard Operating Procedures for every component of your study, link them to the Wiki, and make them publicly available (on Github)
  • Learn to code in R, and establish data cleaning and analysis scripts in R, that you comprehensively annotate and store in Github (linking them to published manuscripts where appropriate)
  • Curate your datasets for public use at the completion of your research project, and where allowed, store them on Github.
  • Meet UNSW’s Research Progress Review milestones, starting with Confirmation of Candidature within your first 9–12 months (for PhD candidates), and subsequent Progress Reviews at the intervals your program requires. You must pass Confirmation to continue in the program, so don’t leave preparation to the last minute — talk to Bridget well ahead of each milestone.
  • Complete UNSW’s required Responsible Conduct of Research (RCR) training as part of your candidature, and keep engaging with it (RCR isn’t a once-off box to tick — see Section 3).
  • Collect data for the lab’s ongoing projects, including any major group projects - data collection is an essential part of your training and you will do a lot of it.
  • Help mentor undergraduate students in the lab.
  • Seek out opportunities to present your work (e.g., at departmental events, in other labs, at conferences).
  • Seek feedback from Bridget and the lab before presenting your research, and take that feedback onboard.
  • Engage in networking opportunities (e.g., attend other lab meetings, volunteer as a student representative at conferences).
  • Apply for scholarships and grants beyond your base Research Training Program (RTP) scholarship where eligible — e.g., top-up scholarships, travel grants, or an [APA equivalent / UNSW-specific top-up, see Bridget]. Writing grant or scholarship applications is an excellent exercise for finding direction in your research.
  • Think critically about what you want for your career (academia — research or teaching, industry, science communication, something else). It is never too early to start discussing this with me (Bridget) so we can make sure you’re getting the training you need.
  • Make sure you meet all UNSW Graduate Research School deadlines (e.g., milestones, annual progress reports, thesis submission) — and make sure Bridget is aware of them.
  • Prioritise time for research. Coursework and any tutoring/demonstrating work are important, but it’s the research that matters for the PhD.

2.5 Lab Managers

All of the above, and you will also be expected to…

  • Collect data for the ongoing group projects in the lab.
  • Manage the lab’s data backup and ensure backups happen on a consistent, documented schedule (see Section on Data Access & Management).
  • Optional: work on your own research (developed with Bridget’s help) — a review paper, analysis of an existing dataset, a study within the group project, or another project outside it.
  • Create research and lab protocols.
  • Train new lab members on research and lab protocols.
  • Monitor adherence to research and lab protocols.
  • Hire, train, mentor, and assess progress of undergraduate research assistants, including setting them up with lab access, making sure they’re properly onboarded, and making sure they’re added to the ethics approvals for the projects they’re working on.
  • Schedule and manage the work of undergraduate research assistants.
  • Maintain human research ethics applications for the lab (writing them, renewing them via the annual/periodic reporting UNSW’s HREAP process requires), archive old consent forms, and keep required paperwork up to date and organised.
  • Maintain the lab website and wiki, update the lab manual, add lab events to shared calendars, manage the lab’s shared drive, and check and respond to the lab’s shared email/inbox.
  • Give new lab members access to the wiki, GitHub, shared calendars, and add their experiments to the shared drive.
  • Assist with recruitment and scheduling of participants for the group project and smaller lab projects.
  • Help manage grant budgets and start-up funds, in line with UNSW’s procurement and financial delegation rules.
  • Monitor lab supplies (e.g., toner, paper, participant incentives) and order more when needed.
  • Ensure lab computers are never updated in the middle of a study.
  • Casual and part-time lab managers/RAs are paid under UNSW’s Professional Staff or Academic Staff Enterprise Agreement, which includes a 25% casual loading on the base hourly rate for casual roles — see https://www.unsw.edu.au/human-resources/our-pay-conditions for current rates.

2.6 Enrolled Research Interns (course credit)

All of the above, and you will also be expected to…

  • Develop your own research project in the lab.
  • Complete your work in a timely manner, back it up, and file it appropriately on the lab’s shared drive (generally, until you have final versions, this will be in your personal folder on Sharepoint).
  • Make sure your work is accessible to anyone — comment your code, name files appropriately (see wiki for lab file-naming conventions), document all aspects of your project, and create a wiki page with all important information.
  • Meet regularly with Bridget or your assigned mentor to discuss your project.
  • Relay updates and project progress to study coordinators and/or the lab manager.
  • Prioritise attending lab meetings when they fit your schedule.
  • Attend all other scheduled RA meetings.
  • Once you’ve finalised a piece of work (e.g., a script, a wiki page), send it to the lab manager for approval. Once approved, the lab manager will copy it to the main study folder.
  • Note that if you’d also like to work as a research assistant in the lab, you must meet with Bridget first, and understand that those duties require a separate weekly time commitment (on top of your research project work).

2.7 Research Assistants

All of the above, and you will also be expected to…

  • Assist other lab members with data collection and analysis.
  • Work on any projects your lab mentor has given you.
  • Complete your work in a timely manner, back it up, and file it appropriately on the lab’s shared drive.
  • Make sure your work is accessible to anyone — leave notes explaining what was done, and make sure spreadsheets and any colour coding are properly annotated.
  • Develop your weekly schedule by talking to your HDR or postdoc mentor. You should be coming in every week and scheduling enough time to get your work done.
  • Relay updates and project progress to study coordinators and/or the lab manager.
  • Prioritise attending lab meetings when they fit your schedule.
  • Attend all other scheduled RA meetings.
  • Keep a log of the work you did in the lab, the dates you were there, and the hours you worked. Send this to your lab mentor when you finish up — they’ll use it to write your reference letter.

2.8 Pathways to Mentorship

Pathways to RAship

  • Major and research interest
  • Time commitment: 10 hours per week
  • Must be able to work after-hours and weekends
  • Interested in a long-term commitment (12 months minimum)
  • Second/third-year undergraduate (preferred)
  • Relevant skill and experience
  • Special consideration for: (1) transfer/articulation students, (2) students with fewer opportunities
