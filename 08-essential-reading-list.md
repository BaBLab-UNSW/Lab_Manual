# Section 8: Essential Reading List

Here are some papers (and a couple of books) that have shaped how we think about brain–body development. For new lab members: read what's relevant to your project first, then work through the rest as you have time.

## 1. Brain–Gut Axis and Mental Health

| Citation | Notes |
|---|---|
| Sudo, N. et al. (2004). [Postnatal microbial colonization programs the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal system for stress response in mice](https://doi.org/10.1113/jphysiol.2004.063388). *The Journal of Physiology*. | An earlier paper in the field that was really influential. It showed that germ-free mice have an elevated 'stress' response, and colonizing them with bacteria early in life normalizes many of their different indices — timing of colonization matters, which suggests a sensitive period for the impacts of the microbiome on brain development. |
| Cryan, J.F. & Dinan, T.G. (2012). [Mind-altering microorganisms: the impact of the gut microbiota on brain and behavior](https://doi.org/10.1038/nrn3346). *Nature Reviews Neuroscience*. | Still the most-cited overview in the field. The figures are especially nice and I often use them in talks to demonstrate the pathways of brain–gut communication. |
| O'Mahony, S.M. et al. (2009). [Early life stress alters behavior, immunity, and microbiota in rats: implications for irritable bowel syndrome and psychiatric illnesses](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.biopsych.2008.06.026). *Biological Psychiatry*. | Maternal separation alters the microbiome, not just behavior, as well as immunity. This paper was the first time I realized that the same stressors which alter the pace of brain development and increase anxious behaviors in mice are also models for irritable bowel syndrome. |
| Diaz Heijtz, R. et al. (2011). [Normal gut microbiota modulates brain development and behavior](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1010529108). *PNAS*. | Companion piece to Sudo. Microbiota shape brain gene expression and behavior, and there's a window early in development when this matters most. |
| Clarke, G. et al. (2013). [The microbiome-gut-brain axis during early life regulates the hippocampal serotonergic system in a sex-dependent manner](https://doi.org/10.1038/mp.2012.77). *Molecular Psychiatry*. | Early microbiome effects on hippocampal serotonin differ by sex. |
| Bercik, P. et al. (2011). [The intestinal microbiota affect central levels of brain-derived neurotrophic factor and behavior in mice](https://doi.org/10.1053/j.gastro.2011.04.052). *Gastroenterology*. | Transferring microbiota (fecal microbiome transplant – FMT) changes behavior and brain in mice. |
| Tillisch, K. et al. (2013). [Consumption of fermented milk product with probiotic modulates brain activity](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23474283/). *Gastroenterology*. | This paper came out right before Bridget started her postdoc at Columbia. It was an exciting proof of concept for the use of neuroimaging and microbiome manipulations together. |
| Cryan, J.F. et al. (2019). [The microbiota-gut-brain axis](https://doi.org/10.1152/physrev.00018.2018). *Physiological Reviews*. | Huge. Don't try to read it cover to cover — treat it as a reference for whenever you need a citation for something specific. |
| Vuong, H.E., Yano, J.M., Fung, T.C. & Hsiao, E.Y. (2017). [The Microbiome and Host Behavior](https://doi.org/10.1146/annurev-neuro-072116-031347). *Annual Review of Neuroscience*. | Another solid comprehensive review, this one leans a bit more into social behavior and neurodevelopment and is actually my favorite review paper on the topic. |
| Cowan, C.S.M., Dinan, T.G. & Cryan, J.F. (2020). [Annual Research Review: Critical windows – the microbiota-gut-brain axis in neurocognitive development](https://doi.org/10.1111/jcpp.13156). *Journal of Child Psychology and Psychiatry*. | Makes the case that microbiome development and brain development share sensitive periods. Ties a lot of the above together. |
| Valles-Colomer, M. et al. (2019). [The neuroactive potential of the human gut microbiota](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41564-018-0337-x)* Nature Microbiology*. | This paper describes the brain-gut module - a method for narrowing the space for metagenomic pathways of potential interest for psychologists/psychiatrists |

**Books** (there are many, but these two are my favorite)

| Citation | Notes |
|---|---|
| Yong, E. (2016). [*I Contain Multitudes: The Microbes Within Us and a Grander View of Life*](https://www.harpercollins.com/products/i-contain-multitudes-ed-yong). Ecco/HarperCollins. | Ed Yong is excellent — I love all his books, and this one I love the best. Great entry into thinking about the wonder of the microbiome. |
| Enders, G. (2015). [*Gut: The Inside Story of Our Body's Most Underrated Organ*](https://www.greystonebooks.com/products/gut). Greystone Books. | Breezier read, mostly digestion and gut anatomy, touches on the brain connection near the end. Good starting point for entering the field. |

## 2. Adversity and the Microbiome

| Citation | Notes |
|---|---|
| Callaghan, B.L., Fields, A., Gee, D.G., Gabard-Durnam, L., Caldera, C., Humphreys, K.L., Goff, B., Flannery, J., Telzer, E.H., Shapiro, M. & Tottenham, N. (2020). [Mind and gut: Associations between mood and gastrointestinal distress in children exposed to adversity](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0954579419000087). *Development and Psychopathology*. | One of the first studies to connect childhood adversity, gut microbiome disruption, and brain activity in kids, using an adoption sample. It is a proof-of-concept study in a small sample — so take it with a grain of salt. But this is basically the paper that kicked off this line of research in the lab. |
| Reid, B.M., Horne, R., Donzella, B., Szamosi, J.C., Coe, C.L., Foster, J.A. & Gunnar, M.R. (2021). [Microbiota-immune alterations in adolescents following early life adversity: A proof of concept study](https://doi.org/10.1002/dev.22061). *Developmental Psychobiology*. | Takes the adversity–microbiome story into adolescence, and adds an immune angle. |
| Hantsoo, L., Jašarević, E., Criniti, S., McGeehan, B., Tanes, C., Sammel, M.D., Elovitz, M.A., Compher, C., Wu, G. & Epperson, C.N. (2019). [Childhood adversity impact on gut microbiota and inflammatory response to stress during pregnancy](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6349044/). *Brain, Behavior, and Immunity*. | Same idea, but in pregnant women. Childhood adversity is linked to a different gut microbiome composition and a different stress response, years later, during pregnancy. |

## 3. Interoception, Mental Health, and the Theory of Constructed Emotion

| Citation | Notes |
|---|---|
| Craig, A.D. (2002). [How do you feel? Interoception: the sense of the physiological condition of the body](https://doi.org/10.1038/nrn894). *Nature Reviews Neuroscience*. | I have only skimmed these but Paul cites a lot — Paul, what do you think? |
| Atzil, S. et al. (2017). [Growing a social brain](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31346259/). *Nature Human Behaviour*. | Excellent paper describing how interoception is built up through our interactions in the social world from the earliest stages of life. This paper challenges traditional views of attachment as being driven by the 'secure base'. |
| Murphy, J. et al. (2017). [Interoception and psychopathology – a developmental neuroscience perspective](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28081519/). *Developmental Cognitive Neuroscience*. | Great review on how to think about interoception across the lifespan (including pregnancy). |
| Barrett, L.F. et al. (2016). [An active inference theory of allostasis and interoception in depression](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5062100/). *Phil. Trans. R. Soc. B*. | Great account of the locked-in model of depression. |
| Barrett, L.F. & Simmons, W.K. (2015). [Interoceptive predictions in the brain](https://doi.org/10.1038/nrn3950). *Nature Reviews Neuroscience*. | The predictive-coding mechanics behind Barrett's theory. |
| Seth, A.K. & Friston, K.J. (2016). [Active interoceptive inference and the emotional brain](https://doi.org/10.1098/rstb.2016.0007). *Phil. Trans. R. Soc. B*. | A parallel account from a different lab. |
| Barrett, L.F. (2017). [The theory of constructed emotion: an active inference account of interoception and categorization](https://doi.org/10.1093/scan/nsw154). *Social Cognitive and Affective Neuroscience*. | The Theory of Constructed Emotion. If you are like me you will need to read this multiple times, but it's worth understanding — it shapes a lot of the ways we are asking questions (or at least trying to) in the lab now. |
| Interoception Summit 2016 participants / Khalsa, S.S. et al. (2018). [Interoception and Mental Health: A Roadmap](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.bpsc.2017.12.004). *Biological Psychiatry: Cognitive Neuroscience and Neuroimaging*. | This was the offshoot of a conference on interoception and is a great paper for linking interoception and mental health. |
| Garfinkel, S.N., Seth, A.K., Barrett, A.B., Suzuki, K. & Critchley, H.D. (2015). [Knowing your own heart: distinguishing interoceptive accuracy from interoceptive awareness](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.biopsycho.2014.11.004). *Biological Psychology*. | Defines accuracy, sensibility, and awareness as separate constructs — this is essential reading for people studying interoception in the lab. |
| Savoca, P.W. et al. (under review). Understanding Interoceptive Modeling and Categorization Following Early-Life Adversity. | Defines two distinct constructs of interoception: interoceptive modeling (the process via which the nervous system predicts and tracks sensory information from the periphery to generate a model of the current or future state of the body) and interoceptive categorization (the categorization and interpretation of that information). |

## 4. Pregnancy and Intergenerational Mental Health

| Citation | Notes |
|---|---|
| Barker, D.J.P. (1995). [The fetal origins of adult disease](https://doi.org/10.1098/rspb.1995.0173). *Proceedings of the Royal Society B*. | The original DOHaD paper. Not about mental health — about birth weight and cardiovascular disease. But this is a really important paper for the field. |
| Entringer, S., Buss, C. & Wadhwa, P.D. (2015). [Prenatal stress, development, health and disease risk: a psychobiological perspective](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4674548/). *Psychoneuroendocrinology*. | A good review on prenatal stress and offspring biological outcomes across a range of domains. |
| Yehuda, R. & Lehrner, A. (2018). [Intergenerational transmission of trauma effects: putative role of epigenetic mechanisms](https://doi.org/10.1002/wps.20568). *World Psychiatry*. | Yehuda has done a lot of studies on intergenerational transmission: holocaust survivors, combat veterans, the 9/11 cohort, and all of that research is reviewed here. |
| Dias, B.G. & Ressler, K.J. (2014). [Parental olfactory experience influences behavior and neural structure in subsequent generations](https://doi.org/10.1038/nn.3594). *Nature Neuroscience*. | The mouse version of intergenerational transmission. Threat-condition a smell in one generation, and the grandchildren who were never exposed to that smell are reactive to it too. |
| Callaghan, B.L., Cowan, C.S.M. & Richardson, R. (2016). [Treating generational stress: Effect of paternal stress on development of memory and extinction in offspring is reversed by probiotic treatment](https://doi.org/10.1177/0956797616653103). *Psychological Science*. | Rats exposed to maternal separation as infants show long-lasting memories of cues tied to shock (unusual — they typically forget really quickly). Those male rats, as adults, sire a new generation, and those pups also show the longer-lasting memories of threat-related stimuli, despite not being directly exposed to stressors themselves. Giving the dads or the second-generation pups probiotics gets rid of that intergenerational transmission of threat behavior. Directly ties the microbiome into intergenerational transmission. |

## 5. Perinatal Health

| Citation | Notes |
|---|---|
| Glynn, L.M., Howland, M.A. & Fox, M. (2018). [Maternal programming: Application of a developmental psychopathology perspective](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0954579418000524). *Development and Psychopathology*. | Argues that pregnancy is a sensitive period for the mother's brain too, not just the baby's. Reframes a conversation that's usually all about the fetus. |
| Monk, C., Lugo-Candelas, C. & Trumpff, C. (2019). [Prenatal Developmental Origins of Future Psychopathology: Mechanisms and Pathways](https://doi.org/10.1146/annurev-clinpsy-050718-095539). *Annual Review of Clinical Psychology*. | Proposes prenatal maternal distress as a third route (beyond genes and parenting) by which risk gets passed down. Good overview of the HPA, immune, placental, and fetal brain mechanisms involved. |
| Savoca, P.W., Glynn, L.M., Fox, M.M., Richards, M.C. & Callaghan, B.L. (2024). [Interoception in pregnancy: Implications for peripartum depression](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neubiorev.2024.105874). *Neuroscience & Biobehavioral Reviews*. | Paul's paper. A framework paper connecting interoception to peripartum depression risk. Read it after you've gone through the interoception papers. |

## 6. Oral Microbiome

| Citation | Notes |
|---|---|
| Gancz, N.N., Querdasi, F.R., Chu, K.A., Towner, E., Taylor, E. & Callaghan, B.L. (2024). [Characteristics of the oral microbiome in youth exposed to caregiving adversity](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11401114/). *Brain, Behavior, & Immunity – Health*. | Naomi's paper. Adversity-exposed kids have a different oral microbiome, and it's less 'reactive' (i.e., has less of a relationship) to cortisol than in comparison kids. |
| Simpson, C.A., Adler, C., du Plessis, M.R., Landau, E.R., Dashper, S.G., Reynolds, E.C., Schwartz, O.S. & Simmons, J.G. (2020). [Oral microbiome composition, but not diversity, is associated with adolescent anxiety and depression symptoms](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.physbeh.2020.113126). *Physiology & Behavior*. | Not adversity-focused, but a useful methodological reference for how oral microbiome studies in adolescents tend to be designed. |
