# Section 4: Use of AI in the Lab

Generative AI tools (large language models, coding assistants, transcription/translation tools, etc.) are now a normal part of research work, and we want lab members to use them well rather than either avoiding them out of caution or leaning on them uncritically. The guiding principle: **AI can help you work, but it cannot do your thinking for you, and it never reduces your responsibility for what you submit.**

### What's generally fine

- Using AI to search literature, summarise papers, or get oriented in an unfamiliar area — as a starting point, not a substitute for reading the primary sources yourself.
- Using AI coding assistants to write, debug, or explain code, provided you understand every line well enough to explain it to someone else and have verified the output does what you think it does (see Section 3 on reproducible research — "I don't know why it works" is not an acceptable answer for analysis code).
- Using AI for grammar, clarity, or copyediting on drafts you wrote.
- Using AI to help draft routine, non-sensitive material — e.g., a first pass at a recruitment flyer, an email template, a slide outline.
- Using AI to help with formatting, restructuring, or generating boilerplate (references in a particular style, a data dictionary skeleton, regex, etc.).

### What's not fine

- Using AI to generate, fabricate, or "clean up" data or results. This is research misconduct — see Section 3 — regardless of whether AI was involved.
- Pasting unpublished data, participant-identifiable information, consent forms, or draft manuscripts under review into a consumer AI tool that isn't covered by a UNSW data protection agreement (see Confidentiality below).
- Letting an AI tool write substantive sections of a paper, grant, or thesis without disclosure and without you having fully verified the content and claims. You are responsible for every citation, number, and claim in anything with your name on it — AI tools regularly invent plausible-sounding references and mis-state findings, and "the AI made it up" is not a defence.
- Listing an AI tool as an author. It cannot take responsibility for the work, cannot give consent to being listed, and does not meet any authorship standard we use (APA, CRediT, or UNSW's own policy — see Section 3).
- Using AI-generated text or analysis in place of your own understanding when you're being assessed on that understanding (e.g., HDR milestones, coursework).

### Confidentiality and data protection

This is the part that matters most given we work with children's and families' data. UNSW has activated **Microsoft Copilot with Commercial Data Protection** for everyone with a zID — this is currently the only generative AI tool that's appropriate for anything involving UNSW research data, because inputs aren't used to train external models and are covered by UNSW's data agreements. Consumer tools (a personal ChatGPT/Claude/Gemini account, browser extensions, random "AI note-taker" apps) are not appropriate for anything containing participant data, even de-identified data, unless you've specifically checked with Bridget and the lab manager first. When in doubt, treat it like you would any other cloud service you haven't vetted — don't put lab data into it.

### Disclosure

If you use AI tools in a way that materially shaped a piece of work (drafting, analysis, figure generation), disclose it — in the manuscript's methods/acknowledgements per the target journal's policy, in your thesis per UNSW's requirements, and to Bridget before it goes out the door. This isn't about penalising AI use; it's the same transparency logic as the rest of Section 3 — if someone needs to know how a result was produced in order to evaluate or reproduce it, tell them.

If you're ever unsure whether a particular use is fine, ask Bridget or your mentor before proceeding rather than after — this is a fast-moving area and the answer may be "let's think about this one."
